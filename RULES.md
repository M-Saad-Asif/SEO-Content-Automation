# RULES — writing, accuracy and SEO

Load once per run. `seo.py lint` checks most of these mechanically; the rest are yours to judge. Your only inputs are the brief, this file and the run's data file: `write_brief.md` in the default (fast) run, `writer_pack.md` in a deep run. The rules hold for any kind of site: a shop, a service business, a publisher, a SaaS company, a public body.

## 0. The page's job (read the writer pack's "Page job" first)

1. The page type decides what the page is for. A **service, product, category, location, home or company page** answers the searcher and makes the client's offer: within the first two paragraphs the reader knows what the client does about their need (in the client's voice or by name) and sees its next step; the page closes with that step. The sections follow the Page job's structure for the type.
2. Official bodies, regulators and other businesses are the **evidence** for rules, fees and times: cite each where its fact is used. On commercial pages, the main offer and CTA belong to the client. Explain official actions the client cannot perform (identity checks, account access, granted-status checks, statutory payments) accurately when the reader needs them.
3. Explain the client's verified value. Avoid repeated directions to buy or apply elsewhere and repeated warnings that displace the offer. Keep material limitations, optional-service disclosures, service and authority fees, direct routes and who makes the decision accurate. Consolidate duplicate disclosures; never remove a necessary warning to protect a sale. Do not imply the client can speed up, influence or replace an authority's decision.
4. Describe the client from the scoped `offer:N` facts, approved claims and client-page facts, paraphrased in its voice. A statement on a client page proves what the client publishes, not an independently proven outcome. Keep its product, route, market and conditions; never transfer one country's processing promise to another. Never invent speed, success rates, guarantees or proof.
5. **Guides, spokes, hubs and product guides** answer their informational, navigational or comparison task. A selling site does not automatically require a sales opening or a brand section. Mention a client offer briefly only when it is verified and relevant. Official workflows can be the main answer on support or status-check guides.
6. Commercial structure follows the Page job. Group questions in relevant sections or a compact FAQ with H3s; do not turn a product, category, company or service page into a sequence of PAA headings. Guide question headings remain useful where they match the task.

## 1. Accuracy

1. Every number, size, price, fee, date, time limit, deadline, official body, product feature and interface label (a setting name, a button) must appear in the run's facts (authority, page-one `serp`, client). If it isn't there, write around it. Don't estimate or fill in numbers from memory.
2. Empty beats fake. A table cell holds a verified fact, or the row or column goes. Never write filler like "Varies", "TBD" or "N/A".
3. Compute; don't reason by eye. Fits, limits, thresholds, counts and rankings come from the pack's computed lines. You may also work out new numbers from facts (a worked example, a total, a per-kg figure): keep the numbers it comes from in the same paragraph and say it the way a person would ("at 30 g per unit, 20 units need about 600 g, so two batches of 300 g"), never as a formula in brackets. Round a worked figure to the precision of its source: a rough guide gives "about 80", not "80.5".
4. No absolutes ("always", "never", "all", "guarantee", "100%", "instant") unless the fact itself says so.
5. Credit each rule to whoever sets it: the regulator sets the legal limit, the manufacturer sets the warranty, the app's maker names its settings. Don't credit a body that didn't make the rule.
6. Say what the client offers only from the client's own published words (client facts and approved claims). Never use a banned claim. Never claim guarantees, special access or influence over someone else's decision.
7. When the answer depends on a condition (a type, a size class, an age band, a country), the sentence names the condition. Don't compress two cases into one rule. A rule from one product's guide (a maker's chart for its own product, an app's own settings) applies to that product: say so, don't present it as general.
8. YMYL pages (health, money, law, safety): every factual statement maps to a fact; give the cautions the authorities give (who should check with a professional, upper limits, risks); and when you tell the reader to do something that costs money (a fee, a service, a lost benefit), say what it costs and when it is worth it. Don't invent a cost line where there is no real cost.
9. Give each product or option at least one honest limitation derived from the data. Never invent a scenario.
10. Usable facts: verified, captured and operator facts, and `serp` facts (points the page-one pages make, section 1.25). A pending, `serp_overridden` or conflicting value is never stated.
11. Don't state prices unless the pack says prices may be stated.
12. Counts and superlatives come from the pack ("Counts to copy", rankings). Never call something the lightest, cheapest, only or most popular unless the pack says so.
13. Use the page's locale: its units (en-US: °F, inches, pounds first, metric in brackets; elsewhere metric), its everyday words (the locale's word for the thing, not another country's), currency, spelling and authorities. Another country's rules appear only when the query or a heading asks about that country.
14. Never copy eight or more words in a row from a source unless you put them in quotation marks and say who wrote them. Paraphrase in your own words.
15. A statistic (a percentage, a survey result, an average return, a market figure) carries its original source and year in the sentence ("a 2019 study by <organisation> found…"). If the pack only has it second-hand (a blog or roundup that doesn't name the study and year), verify the original in step 2 or leave it out. A figure more than five years old appears only as history, never as how things are now.
16. Keep the strength the source gives a consequence. If it says illegal, replace, stop or see a doctor, don't soften it to "have it checked" or "consider".
17. A table covers the range readers bring (every common size, age or plan). If the source stops short, say where the figures stop and what readers outside them should do.
18. A source written for a particular group (patients with a condition, professionals, one product's buyers, one country) applies to that group. Check the source's title in the pack before you use it for general advice, and name the group when you do use it.
19. A comparison ("as much as", "more than", "the main factor", "most people") needs a source that makes that comparison, and must agree with the page's own numbers.
20. Answer each question truthfully even when the honest answer is "it depends" or "no": never force a yes to fit a heading.
21. A step does only what its source says it does. Never present a method as fixing something its source says it doesn't touch; if no method exists, say so.
22. A client claim keeps its conditions: "free delivery on orders over £X" never becomes "free delivery". Counts and figures about the client come from the approved claims, never from an older page. When the page explains a condition or a cost that applies to every product of this kind (flexible or not, fees, minimums, notice periods, warranty terms), say how the client's own product stands on it, from its pages, and give every cost layer its fee page lists, not only the headline one.
23. Credit a legal duty to the law that sets it (the regulation or act, as the source names it), not to the body's guidance about it.
24. A fact's scope travels with it: when the pack or dossier says a fact applies to a group, product, version or year ("applies to: …"), the sentence that uses it names that scope or stays within it.
25. **Page one is validated evidence.** Google already ranks the page-one results for this query in this market, so a point they make (`serp` facts) is stated as fact without a separate research trip. Your own knowledge is the sanity check: leave out a point you know is wrong or outdated, and say so in your closing line; never "research" it. When page-one pages contradict each other, the higher-ranked page wins (the brief lists the resolved contradictions; never use a losing value). A verified authority fact beats any competitor. Every point page one covers belongs on this page unless it is off the page's job: missing one is lost information. The questions searchers ask that page one leaves unanswered (People Also Ask, related searches) are the information gain: answer each.

## 2. Voice: write as the expert, not as a summary of sources

- State verified facts in your own words, as the page's author. Put the source link on the claim itself (an inline link on the words the reader would want to check) or in the sources line at the end.
- Name a source in the sentence only when the reader needs to know who says it: an opinion, a study's finding, a surprising or contested claim, or who sets a rule. Keep it to about one named attribution per section. "According to X… X adds… X notes…" in every paragraph reads as a research paste.
- Give judgement. For every decision the reader faces, say what you recommend and why, reasoned from the facts: which figure to aim for, which option suits whom, when to stop and get help. The reasoning is yours; the numbers come from the pack.
- Add something the top pages don't have: a worked example built from the facts, a comparison table, a decision rule, the client's own data. That is the page's information gain.
- When you explain a check or a test, give every outcome: what passing looks like, what failing looks like, and what to do next in each case, in plain sentences ("If the paint drags, wait for a warmer day"), never as "Pass:/Fail:" labels.
- Forums and reviews are evidence of experience, never of rules or numbers. Use one only when the pack quotes a real comment from readers like this page's (same country, same situation), link the comment, and attribute it plainly. Never quote a forum without its link, never on a YMYL page as support for advice, and never write "people on Reddit say…" without a quote behind it.
- On informational pages, tie the client in briefly where it is true and useful. On commercial pages, the opening, process, proof and closing action follow section 0; do not limit the offer to one incidental sentence.
- State conditions the way people do: "If you rent, …", not "When you rent, …" for a case that may not apply.
- Write as the client's page. Where the client's products, services or guides help the reader take the next step, link them naturally inside the relevant section (see section 6). Don't end with a list of links.
- Describe the client's own product or service directly, from its approved claims ("Acme sells refurbished laptops with a 12-month warranty"). Never quote the client's own tagline back at the reader in the third person ("X's site invites you to…"), and link a client claim only to the page the pack lists as its source.
- Prefer independent sources (authorities, charities, professional bodies, research) over a competitor of the client for links. A page-one point about the topic (section 1.25) is stated in your own words with no link to the competitor. A fact about a competitor's own product or offer (its price, its feature) is attributed as theirs, limited to their product, never the main answer and never the first link on the page.
- When the client publishes its own method, plan or figures for this question, the page leads with the client's way, in the client's voice; other sources are comparisons or support. Name a source only where it adds weight, not on every step.
- When sources disagree (one says N days, another 2N), say which you recommend and why, in one sentence. Never leave the reader to reconcile them.
- Never mention the research itself ("the top results", "ranking pages", "most guides online"). State the facts in your own words, as the page's author.
- Questions about a competitor's product ("Is <competitor> a …?") are not sections on the client's page. Mention the competitor where a comparison helps, at most.

## 3. Sentences

- Average 15–20 words, with real variation from longer sentences that carry a condition or a reason, not from fragments. Keep sentences under 8 words to about one in ten. Never answer a heading with a verbless fragment. Most sentences stay under 30 words; about one in ten can run to 35–40 when it carries a list, a condition or a contrast clearly.
- The first paragraph under a question heading (and under a question-form H1) is the snippet answer: 40–50 words in 2–3 sentences, answering directly. Supporting detail goes after it and adds to the answer (the how, the numbers, the exceptions); it never restates the answer as a list. The first sentence gives the answer the reader should act on (the recommended time, the figure to aim for), not only the widest range a source allows; the range and exceptions follow. A yes/no question gets the true yes or no inside the first sentence ("No, you can't…"), never as a one-word sentence; open with "Yes,"/"No," in at most two sections and let the others lead with the fact itself.
- No teaser sentences that announce a point instead of making it ("The method has one limit worth knowing.", "Here is a worked example.", "The risk is real."). Make the point.
- Answer in natural language. Never paste the query or a heading into a sentence as an embedded question ("The question of how much X should I buy has no single answer" is wrong).
- Vary how sections open. Lead with the number, the condition, the action or the reason; don't open every section by restating its heading as a statement. Never twist word order to avoid that ("Lower bills are the main benefit of insulation" is an inversion; "Insulation lowers your bills" is natural).
- Name the actor. An opening "-ing", "-ed", "At [number]" or "When/If + participle" phrase must describe the subject of the main clause and relate to its claim. When in doubt, write two plain sentences.
- No trailing commentary clauses (", making it ideal…", ", allowing you to…", ", ensuring…").
- Don't open with a vague "This", "It" or "These" + verb. Name the thing.
- Break noun stacks of three or more modifiers. Use real verbs, not nominalisations ("staff check the limit", not "apply checks on").
- Plain register. No pseudo-legal words (sovereign, statutory, adjudication, substantive, dossier) unless you quote an official term.

## 4. Consistency

- Address the reader as "you". The client is "we" where the pack's voice measurements show the client's own guides use "we" (or on service pages); otherwise use the client's name.
- Don't drift to a role noun for the reader ("users", "customers", or the niche words in the brief's `reader_nouns`).
- Answers are indicative; steps are imperative. Present tense by default.
- Follow the locale spelling and number style (en-GB: colour, organise, centre, "per cent" or "%" consistently). Dimensions use ×: "45 × 36 × 20 cm".
- One name per thing. Use a product's display name on first mention, then a short form.
- One spelling per word across the page ("setup" or "set-up", "email" or "e-mail", never both), following the locale.

## 5. Sound like a person wrote it

- No visible framework labels: "Trade-off:", "Key takeaway", "Verdict:", "Pro tip:", "Bottom line".
- Vary section shapes. Some sections are a short paragraph; one might be a table or steps.
- No meta-commentary about the page: "below", "above", "this guide", "the sections that follow", "as covered earlier".
- No colon reveals ("The catch: …", "X puts it simply: …"), no negative parallelism ("not X, it's Y" and "X rather than Y" together at most twice per page), no rule-of-three padding, no ceremonial intro, no closing summary, no closing moral ("…matters more than the label").
- Use bold sparingly; no "**Label:** text" bullets.
- Avoid the banned and limited words in `scripts/lexicon.yaml` and the brief's `banned_phrases`. Say the specific thing instead of swapping in another inflated word.
- No internal codes, SKUs or supplier references.
- At most one em dash per 150 words. No exclamation marks (a brand name that contains one is fine).

## 6. Structure, headings and links

- One H1. On an existing page, use the live H1 exactly unless the brief sets `h1`. On a new page, write a sentence-case H1 that matches the query.
- Front matter at the top of `draft.md`:

  ```
  ---
  title: <about 60 characters or under>
  meta_description: <140–160 characters>
  author: <only when the pack gives a byline>
  ---
  ```

- The title and meta description promise only what the page delivers ("by size and age" needs figures by age as well as by size), and the meta description doesn't repeat the title.

- Order sections the way the reader needs them and the top pages answer them: the main task or answer first, background and edge cases after. Never end on a definition or background section; the last section is a decision or a next step.
- No H2 may repeat the H1's question in other words ("How much X do I need per day?" under "How much X do I need?"); merge it into the opening. Headings keep one person ("you" or "I") throughout.
- On guides, explanatory H2s are the reader's questions or precise "How to" tasks. Commercial headings name the offer, process, specification or decision; questions can be FAQ H3s. Use sentence case and headings meaningful on their own. Answer each distinct question once: related variants share one heading. Skip a question you can't answer truthfully (record a `Skip:` line in the outline). A heading promises exactly what its section delivers: if the evidence covers a different period, group or measure than the question asks (15 years of data under "over 10 years"), word the heading to what the evidence answers.
- Use tables only for real comparisons where every cell is a fact or a shown calculation, and link the table's source in the sentence that introduces it.
- For a "how to" query, give the main method as numbered steps: what to prepare, each action in order, and how to read the result (what passing and failing look like). Scope stays honest: if the title or meta names a case (a type, a product, a group), the method covers that case or the title drops it.
- Fit the page type (decided from the page itself before research; the pack states it):
  - **service**: the client's offer first, in its own verified words (what it arranges or does, for whom, the headline terms), then how it works, eligibility, costs and timescales from the client's pages, then the questions buyers ask (People Also Ask, competitors' questions) as short answered sections, then the next step. Rules and figures that aren't the client's come from independent sources; the client's own terms lead where they are sound.
  - **location**: a service page for a place: the same, plus only verified local specifics (never invented local detail).
  - **product**: what it is and who it's for, the specification from the product data (a table), what it's good and not good for, sizing or care, buyers' questions, related products.
  - **category**: a short intro that defines the range, how to choose (the criteria that matter, as a table where facts allow), links to the products or sub-categories, buyers' questions.
  - **home**: who the business is and for whom, its core services or ranges each linked, proof from approved claims, the next step.
  - **company** (about, contact): facts about the client only, from its own pages.
  - **guide** complete for the reader's decision; **hub** short, linking child pages; **spoke** specific to one entity or task; **product-guide** products first, each with a data-derived limitation.
- Every URL comes from the "Links you may use" section of the pack. Never guess, shorten or rebuild a URL.
- Link products to their own pages, never to a collection. Link an authority where its rule or figure is used (on a guide, also where the reader acts there; on a commercial page the main CTA belongs to the client while necessary official actions remain accurate). Use descriptive anchors of 2–6 words that name the target page (its title, not your sentence). Link each source page once, on the first sentence that credits it; later claims from it name the source without linking again. When you credit a publisher whose other page you linked more recently, link the page this claim is on: "the RHS says" sends the reader to the nearest RHS link, so that link must be the page that says it. Never link one page more than twice, and contextual client pages once each; a main commercial CTA may appear near the opening and close. Never link the client's existing page on this same query: this page replaces it.
- Put an internal link on the words in a sentence that already says something useful; don't add a sentence whose only job is to carry a link ("X's guide to Y shows how."). Keep internal links to about one per 150 words, and don't end most sections the same way.
- Stay within the word range. A guide should cover what the top pages agree on, so if the draft is well under the pack's median length, check the topic list for gaps before you finish.

## 7. Algorithmic authorship (Koray, harmonised with the rules above)

- **Declaration first.** The first sentence under a heading answers it directly, in natural word order: the thing asked about, the action or the number as subject, whichever a person would say. Put conditions after the declaration unless the answer depends on them (rule 1.7).
- **Certainty with parameters.** State facts plainly; hedge only when the source does, and name the condition. Counts are followed by their members: "two conditions apply: A and B".
- **Define and connect.** Expand acronyms on first use unless they are brand names. Say how entities relate with a precise verb. Cover the pack's must-cover entities with their attributes and values.
- **One macro context, macro to micro.** No generic "Introduction", "Overview" or "Conclusion" headings. A compact FAQ with specific H3 questions is allowed on commercial pages.
- **Paragraphs.** 2–4 sentences, one idea each. List items start with the same part of speech. Number a list only when its items are steps done in order; alternatives (method A, method B or method C) are bullets or a table.
- **Reasons add information.** "Replace it within five years, because it lasts up to five years" restates itself; give the reason that the reader didn't already have, or none.
- **Bold the answer, not the keyword.** At most one bold per section.
- **Serve the task, then support it.** Lead with the answer or relevant offer this page exists to provide. Cover relevant shared topics and verified useful gaps where they help the reader; competitor consensus and order are evidence leads, not instructions that override page purpose.
- **Responsiveness test.** For every H2 question and the target query, you can point to the one sentence that answers it.

## 8. Rankability

- Put the target query's words in the title and the opening paragraph, in natural order and natural grammar.
- Cover each secondary query from the pack in a heading or an opening sentence, only where it reads naturally.
- Link internally to the client's relevant pages, and to the authorities whose rules the page states.

## 8b. Answer engines (AEO and GEO)

AI answers (Google AI Overviews, ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini) quote passages, not pages. Write every answer so it can be lifted on its own and still be right.

- **Self-contained answers.** The first paragraph under a question heading answers it directly; guide snippet answers aim for 40–60 words and makes sense with nothing else around it: name the subject in full ("A stocks and shares ISA…", not "It…" or "This…"), and carry the condition and the figure in the same paragraph.
- **One-sentence definition.** Early on, define the main entity as "X is a Y that Z", in the reader's words, with the attribute that sets it apart.
- **A sourced figure early.** Where the topic has a defining number (a limit, a rate, a dose, an allowance), give it with its source and date in the first 300 words.
- **Quote where wording matters.** For a rule or an official definition, quote the authority's exact words in quotation marks, name it and link it. One or two such quotes a page; everything else in your own words.
- **Dates on anything that changes.** Tax years, rates, prices, limits and rules carry the period they apply to ("for the 2026/27 tax year"). Never write "currently" without the date it was true.
- **YMYL warnings.** On money, health, legal and safety pages, give the warnings a professional in the field must give, from the facts (for example, that an investment's value can fall, that tax treatment depends on circumstances and can change, when to see a doctor or a regulated adviser), once each, inside the section where that decision is made and in concrete terms (what can happen, to whom). No boilerplate lines: "general, not personal advice", "figures as of…" or "speak to an adviser if unsure" as stand-alone closers read as templates; the date goes in the sentence with the figure, and the page's `updated:` date is added for you.

## 9. When facts are missing

Never pause and never ask. Leave the claim out, or say plainly what the reader should check and link the authority (on a commercial page, without making that check the page's advice). The omission is logged in the report as a decision.
